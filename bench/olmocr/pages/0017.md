(b) The axis of tailstock spindle is not in-line with axis of headstock spindle in horizontal and/or vertical planes. The following system is used to determine in which plane the error is located.

Any error caused solely by a misalignment in the horizontal plane can be eliminated by mere mechanical manipulation of the tailstock. First, "mike" the collars, and if the diameter of the collar at the tailstock end is the larger of the two, the lateral adjustment screws are turned to move the tailstock top in its transverse way towards the front of the lathe. On the other hand, if the collar at the tailstock end has a smaller diameter the tailstock is set over towards the back of the lathe. Repeated cuts, measurements, and adjustments are made until the diameters of both collars are identical. In this way all error in the horizontal plane is eliminated.

To determine if misalignment is present in the vertical plane, the following procedure may be adopted. This test is most easily performed by mounting a DIAL INDICATOR on the tool post and adjusting the button to touch one of the turned collars at the vertical diameter. The pointer is set to zero, and then without changing the setting, the carriage is traversed along the bed ways and the reading noted at the other collar. If there is no misalignment in the vertical plane, the reading will be identical. If there is a difference, correction is made by scraping the slides of either the headstock or tailstock, whichever has its axis higher as measured from the bed ways.

CAUTION:
The machining of the two narrow test collars of the same diameter, one at each end of the bar, at a single setting of the cutting tool, is not the infallible test for alignment of the axis of the tailstock spindle to axis of headstock spindle that it is popularly supposed to be. Should the tailstock spindle be either higher or lower than the headstock spindle, it is still possible to machine, without changing the setting of the tool, two narrow test collars of apparently identical diameters by simply adjusting the tailstock in its transverse way. The reason for this phenomenon is that for any particular height of the cutting edge of the tool there will be a certain set-over of the tailstock which will apparently compensate for the misalignment of the tailstock axis in the vertical plane. In reality, the collars will be hyperbolic rather than cylindrical in character. Where the misalignment in the vertical plane is small, variations in micrometer readings on the several diameters will be almost imperceptible. Therefore, in order to make an accurate test of the alignment of the head and tail centers in the vertical plane, the following procedure should be adopted.

Machine two collars at opposite ends of a bar held between centers, using a light cut and fine feed. Measure the collars with a micrometer. If the measurements are not the same, adjust the tailstock transversely, out or in as required, and machine again. Machine and measure consecutively until the collars "mike" identically. Then fasten a DIAL INDICATOR to the tool post of the carriage and adjust the button of the instrument to the vertical diameter of one collar. Traverse the carriage along the bed and observe the DIAL reading at the other collar. If the reading is zero-zero the axes of the head and tail centers are correctly aligned in the vertical plane.

An additional test may be made which is based on the premise that if the headstock spindle and tailstock spindle are correctly aligned to each other in both planes, then the height of the cutting tool above, below, or on-center will produce identical diameters in the two test collars without changing the setting of the tool on the machining cut. Thus after obtaining equal diameters on the two collars by adjusting the tailstock, a second cut is made across both collars, this time with the cutting edge of the tool raised or lowered from its first setting. Any misalignment of the tailstock in the vertical plane will be immediately apparent by a difference in diameters of the two test collars.
